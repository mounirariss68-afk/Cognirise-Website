# UK DSIT — AI Adoption Research

- **Source:** UK Department for Science, Innovation and Technology (DSIT), research conducted by IFF Research and Technopolis Group, *AI Adoption Research*
- **URL:** https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-adoption-research/ai-adoption-research
- **Publication/update date:** 2026-02-13 (the official GOV.UK search result reports this update; fieldwork was in 2025)
- **Accessed:** 2026-09-14
- **Tier:** Tier 1 — government-commissioned primary survey and qualitative interviews

## Design and sample

- Quantitative fieldwork used computer-assisted telephone interviewing from **12 February to 2 May 2025**.
- The study completed **3,500 interviews** with UK private-sector businesses with at least five employees. Interviews sought the person responsible for or overseeing technology.
- Results were weighted by business size and sector. Information and communication, finance, business services and administration were oversampled because they were sectors of interest and were expected to be more likely to use AI.
- A follow-up qualitative component included **100 interviews**, conducted by phone or Microsoft Teams between March and May 2025. Targets included users, prospective adopters and businesses that neither used nor planned to use AI.
- The business sample came from the commercial Market Location database.

The report says this approach does not capture “shadow AI” use, because the respondent may not know about unapproved or informal use.

## Evidence relevant to SME nonadopters and buyers

### The nonadopter pool is large, with a size gradient

- **16%** of surveyed businesses were currently using at least one AI technology, **5%** planned to adopt in the future, and **80%** neither used nor had plans to use AI.
- **51%** did not see AI as relevant to their organisation.
- Current use was reported by **36% of large businesses, 23% of mid-sized businesses and 14% of micro businesses**, compared with 16% overall.
- Construction, retail/distribution, transport/storage and hotel/catering were more likely to be in the “neither use nor have plans” group, and those sectors were also more likely to perceive AI as not relevant.

These are UK survey results, not a rate for SMEs globally. They do, however, directly include smaller businesses and a qualitative nonadopter group.

### Need and skills precede procurement

When all businesses were asked what prevented or had previously prevented AI adoption:

- **71%** selected “we have not identified a use for AI in our organisation.”
- **60%** selected limited AI skills, expertise or knowledge.
- **48%** selected a lack of tools/platforms for developing AI models.
- **29%** selected projects being too complex or difficult to integrate and scale.
- **28%** selected unclear or uncertain regulation.

Among businesses with no plans to adopt AI, the largest barrier was lack of identified need (**81%**), followed by limited AI skills (**59%**) and lack of tools/platforms (**49%**). Among businesses planning to adopt, limited skills (**68%**) and lack of tools/platforms (**54%**) were the most common factors; unclear regulation was also prominent (**43%**).

The interviews explain the “need” finding: several organisations had not identified a compelling business case, so AI felt like a solution in search of a problem. Respondents also described not having the time or confidence to research and evaluate possible tools.

### Cost/value and the business case

The survey asked respondents to rate the significance of barriers they faced. The most significant was ethical concerns (**80%**), followed by high costs (**76%**) and unclear or uncertain regulation (**72%**). The report says high perceived or actual cost was especially difficult for smaller organisations when there was no guaranteed ROI. This is a perceived-risk finding, not evidence that an investment would not pay back.

The qualitative interviews provide concrete decision language:

- One small business described a “quick, easy, cheap test” rather than spending a lot before learning.
- One medium business said an owner would require a case such as **£50,000 of cost to save £100,000 over two years** before signing off. This is one respondent's illustration, not a market benchmark.
- Another respondent said the business needed solutions that benefit the business rather than a “toy.”

Budget evidence shows why a buyer may need a lightweight business case:

- Among businesses planning to adopt AI in the next year, **47% had not budgeted a specific amount** and **25% did not know how much was budgeted**.
- Among businesses that used or planned to use AI and could report 2024 spending, the reported median was **£2,000** and mean **£19,000**. **31%** reported no AI-related spending in 2024, and **35%** did not know how much they had spent. These figures are not SME price guidance; they describe a mixed group and only the subset able to report a figure.

### What value has been observed, and what remains unproven

Among current AI users, the report records self-reported outcomes:

- **75%** reported improved workforce productivity.
- **57%** reported new or improved processes or operations.
- **34%** reported reduced production or operating costs.
- **77%** reported no change in revenue since adopting AI; **12%** reported an increase.

The report explicitly cautions that these impacts are self-reported and may not be completely accurate. The gap between reported operational improvements and limited revenue change supports a buyer need for baseline measures and an evidence plan, but it does not establish causality or ROI.

### What smaller organisations tend to buy or plan

- Among current users, **85%** used natural-language processing/text-generation tools; the report links this to the availability and accessibility of off-the-shelf generative tools.
- Most businesses deployed the technologies studied by buying ready-to-use external solutions rather than developing in-house.
- Interviewees cited lack of knowledge or technical expertise as a reason for choosing ready-to-use systems; some also described them as cheaper than bespoke development.
- Businesses planning adoption reported **34%** feeling ready, **33%** unsure and **32%** not ready to implement AI.
- Planned use cases among businesses using or planning AI were creative/content creation (**77%**), administration/support (**70%**), data/analytics (**56%**) and automation (**47%**). These are not nonadopter needs and should not be read as a prevalence estimate for all UK SMEs.

## Buyer-need synthesis (inference, not a direct DSIT claim)

The study supports a buyer experience that starts with:

1. a sector/workflow relevance test and a named problem;
2. a low-cost, bounded experiment with a decision rule;
3. a transparent total-cost and ROI/evidence model;
4. practical skills, time and external implementation support; and
5. an explicit option to stop when the problem is not material or a non-AI route is better.

The “no AI alternative” need is inferred from respondents who could not see a relevant use, viewed their work as manual or relationship-based, or valued personal service. The study did not compare AI with a specified non-AI intervention.

## Limitations and evidence boundary

- This is a UK study; its percentages should not be generalized to all SMEs, GCC firms or the global market.
- The sample covers private businesses with at least five employees, while Eurostat/OECD comparisons often start at 10 employees; size bands are therefore not interchangeable.
- The survey is weighted by size and sector but oversampled selected sectors; the report's full weighting and response details should be checked before subgroup comparisons.
- Interview findings are qualitative and illustrative. Individual quotes, including the £50,000/£100,000 ROI example, are not prevalence or benchmark data.
- Adoption, impact, spending and readiness are mostly respondent reports. Reported productivity or cost benefits are not independently audited causal outcomes.
- The technology respondent may not know about informal or shadow use.
