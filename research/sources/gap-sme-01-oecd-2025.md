# OECD — AI adoption by small and medium-sized enterprises

- **Source:** Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), *AI adoption by small and medium-sized enterprises: OECD discussion paper for the G7*
- **URL:** https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2025/12/ai-adoption-by-small-and-medium-sized-enterprises_9c48eae6/426399c1-en.pdf
- **Publication date:** 2025-12-09 (the OECD landing-page search result gives this date; the PDF is © OECD 2025)
- **Accessed:** 2026-09-14
- **Tier:** Tier 1 — intergovernmental official evidence and synthesis

## What was studied

This is an OECD discussion paper prepared at the request of the 2025 G7 Canadian Presidency. It combines OECD ICT/business statistics, Eurostat data, OECD surveys, and G7 country case material. The paper says its 2025 D4SME survey covered nearly 1,000 SMEs across six G7 countries; elsewhere it describes an OECD survey covering four G7 countries when reporting skill barriers. The D4SME survey material is explicitly described as non-representative of the full business population.

The paper's cross-country adoption comparisons use the OECD ICT Access and Usage by Businesses database. The report notes that the latest observation differs by country (2020–2024), definitions are not identical, and the database was accessed in July 2025.

## Evidence relevant to SME nonadopters and buyers

### Adoption is lower among smaller firms, and basic use is not the same as core-work transformation

- Across the OECD, the paper reports 2024-or-latest AI use of **11.9% for firms with 10–49 employees**, **20.4% for firms with 50–249 employees**, and **40% for firms with 250 or more employees**.
- In the EU 2024 data used by the paper, the largest size gap was for autonomous robots, self-driving vehicles and drones (**7.2% of large firms versus 0.7% of small firms**). The gap was smaller for natural-language generation (**16.7% versus 4.6%**), which the paper links to the relative accessibility of those tools.
- Among SMEs using generative AI, only **29%** reported using it in core activities; the paper characterises much SME use as peripheral support rather than a radical reshaping of production processes.

These statistics support a distinction between an SME that has tried an accessible assistant and one that has a sufficiently important, measurable workflow to justify deeper investment.

### What adopters say they get

- In the cited OECD SME evidence, improved employee performance was the main reported benefit of generative AI, followed by cost savings and performing new tasks.
- Offering new products/services, competing with larger companies and increasing revenues were reported less often.
- Among SMEs using generative AI that had recently experienced a skill gap, **38%** said generative AI helped compensate for it.
- The report cautions that positive productivity associations do not establish that AI caused the advantage: already more digital and competitive firms are more likely to adopt. It also notes that productivity gains may take time and require complementary investment and organisational change.

For a nonadopter, the evidence therefore points to a need to specify the operational outcome first (for example, employee time or a defined task), rather than treating revenue growth as an automatic result.

### Skills, training and time are material constraints

- In an OECD survey covering four G7 countries, **50% of SMEs** reported that employees lacked the skills to use generative AI.
- In the 2025 D4SME survey, **more than 50% of nearly 1,000 respondents** reported lack of knowledge about how to use generative AI as a barrier. The report gives country variation among the surveyed countries: **80% in Japan, 50% in Canada, and 40% in the United Kingdom and Germany**.
- Internet research, employee knowledge sharing and AI literacy were among the most desired capabilities.
- In the same G7 survey, fewer than 30% of SMEs using generative AI reported that employees participated in AI-related training; the reported range was **11.3% in Japan to 29.4% in Canada**.
- The paper notes that time away from revenue-generating work and higher per-worker training costs are particular problems for smaller firms.

The buyer need suggested by these findings is not only “buy a tool.” It is access to a bounded use case, practical enablement and enough staff time to test and learn.

### Finance and the cost of embedding AI

The OECD identifies finance as one of four critical SME enablers, alongside connectivity, AI-enabling inputs and skills. It says finance may be needed for software and hardware, upskilling, hiring talent and organisational change, with lack of financial resources a recurring digital-transition barrier. The paper also describes tighter credit conditions, higher SME financing costs and a shift toward shorter-term finance as constraints on longer-term technology investment.

This is evidence for an adoption offer that makes total cost visible: licenses or compute, integration/data work, staff time, training, monitoring and changes to processes. It is not evidence for a universal SME budget or an AI price benchmark.

### “No AI use” and government support differ by country

In the paper's Table 4, non-users of generative AI were asked whether they agreed with possible reasons for not using it. The top barrier differed by country:

| Surveyed country | Main reported barrier | Main requested support |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Canada | Generative AI is not suited to the type of work the company does | Training |
| Germany | Clients would not approve of the company using generative AI | Information campaigns |
| Japan | Employees do not have the right skills to use generative AI | Financial assistance |
| United Kingdom | Generative AI is not suited to the type of work the company does | Training |

The table is a country-specific summary of the surveyed respondents, not a global ranking. The paper also reports that **86% of surveyed SMEs** had a neutral or favourable company attitude toward generative AI, so attitude alone did not appear to explain non-use in that sample.

“Not suited to the type of work” is the closest current OECD evidence to a no-AI alternative: it indicates that some buyers need an honest relevance test and permission to choose not to automate. The study did not directly randomise or compare an AI intervention with a non-AI alternative.

## Buyer-need synthesis (inference, not a direct OECD claim)

For SME nonadopters, a credible first exchange should help them:

1. identify a specific business problem before naming an AI solution;
2. test whether the problem is relevant enough to justify AI, or whether a simpler non-AI approach is better;
3. estimate full cost and time-to-evidence, including skills, training, integration and ongoing finance;
4. show a measurable operational benefit before promising revenue or scale; and
5. build enough internal knowledge for staff to evaluate, use and govern the result.

These are research-informed buyer needs, not validated demand categories or a claim that every SME needs the same service.

## Limitations and evidence boundary

- The paper is a current official synthesis, not a representative 2025/2026 interview study of all SME buyers.
- The D4SME evidence is non-representative, spans selected G7 countries, and combines survey years and country samples. The country percentages above must not be pooled into a market-wide rate.
- Adoption comparisons use latest-available observations with country-year and definition differences; they are not a single contemporaneous global estimate.
- The report's productivity associations and cited potential gains are not causal ROI evidence.
- The country table measures stated reasons and preferred support. It does not show whether a buyer approved spending, completed implementation or achieved a financial return.
- The paper supports “not suited/not useful” as a relevance concern, but does not test AI against a defined non-AI alternative.
