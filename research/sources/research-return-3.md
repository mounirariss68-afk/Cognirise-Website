## Key Facts

- **Pilots stall at the business-case/measurement boundary.** Cisco’s 2025 survey of 8,039 senior AI leaders found that only 32% had a process to measure AI impact; only 34% felt very confident monetizing use cases, and only one-third had a formal change-management plan. Wharton’s 2025 U.S. enterprise survey shows the transition toward accountability: 72% reported formal ROI tracking, but security risk, operational complexity, inaccurate outputs, privacy, and regulation remained leading barriers. These are self-reported survey results, not audited ROI ([Cisco evidence](research/sources/evidence/ai-production-results-02-cisco.md), [Wharton evidence](research/sources/evidence/ai-production-results-03-wharton.md)).

- **Buyer situation 1 — exploring AI but unable to build the business case (mandatory).**
  - **Trigger:** leadership pressure to “do something with AI” or show tangible ROI, while staff have experiments, tool usage, or demos but no agreed investable use case. Cisco reports rising urgency to demonstrate ROI; RAND’s practitioners say leadership often specifies the wrong problem or metric.
  - **Existing assets to inventory:** experiments and candidate workflows, available data, baseline KPIs, business owner, security constraints, and any internal AI usage. The key asset gap to test is not “AI interest” but a defensible link from one workflow to measurable business impact.
  - **Blocked decision:** approve a funded pilot/production path, or decide which use case deserves one.
  - **Desired outcome:** a business-case-ready use case with baseline, metric, owner, data feasibility, integration/risk cost, and an explicit continue/stop decision.
  - **Distinguishing signal:** unlike the roadmap buyer below, this buyer has not yet commissioned or accepted a strategy/roadmap; the blocked decision is **which problem and why now**, not how to execute an already-approved portfolio.

- **Buyer situation 2 — strategy/roadmap already purchased, next step unclear (mandatory).**
  - **Trigger:** a paid AI strategy or roadmap has been delivered and discussed by executives, but no one has converted it into a first implementation, accountable product owner, production integration plan, or operating budget.
  - **Existing assets to inventory:** the roadmap and prioritization logic, executive sponsor, architecture/data assessment, existing vendors, candidate use cases, and any promised KPI or risk assumptions. Wharton finds strategy responsibilities increasingly consolidated in internal teams, while external consultants remain a rollout option; McKinsey’s GCC evidence separates strategic intent from the talent/operating model, technology/data, change management, and workflow redesign needed to execute it.
  - **Blocked decision:** select the first use case and delivery owner; define what the next statement of work or internal build must produce; authorize integration and run/maintenance spend.
  - **Desired outcome:** a roadmap-to-delivery handoff that names the first accountable business/technical team, target system of record, data rights, controls, KPI, production-readiness criteria, and post-launch owner.
  - **Distinguishing signal:** the strategy artifact and executive direction already exist; the unmet need is **operationalization and ownership**, not discovery of AI value. This is distinct from the exploratory buyer even when both say “we need an AI pilot.”

- **Pilot-to-production is an environment and workflow problem, not only a model problem.** IBM describes pilots as using curated data and manual review; production introduces distributed data, inconsistent business definitions, continuous governance, and the need for outputs to update systems of record. RAND’s 65-practitioner study documents test/production incompatibility, missing deployment automation, weak monitoring, inadequate data infrastructure, and fast-changing priorities. The production buyer’s assets are therefore a working POC, curated dataset, manual validation process, and target workflow; the blocked decision is production authorization/integration; the desired outcome is a reliable, monitored system that can act within existing controls ([IBM evidence](research/sources/evidence/ai-production-results-06-ibm.md), [RAND evidence](research/sources/evidence/ai-production-results-01-rand.md)).

- **Buyer situation 3 — existing deployment is disappointing.** TechTarget reports that McDonald’s stopped testing IBM automated order-taking in restaurants in July 2024 after dialect and accuracy problems. It also reports Taco Bell/Yum voice ordering errors in noisy drive-throughs, repeated upselling, and continuing questions about consistency, customer experience, and long-term ROI even as deployments expanded. This is a different starting point from a stuck pilot: there is a live or near-live asset, observed failure data, user/customer impact, and a continue/repair/rollback decision ([TechTarget evidence](research/sources/evidence/ai-production-results-08-techtarget.md)). The McDonald’s event is older than 18 months as of 2026-09-14 and should not be treated as a current prevalence signal.

- **GCC-specific signal:** McKinsey’s August–September 2025 survey of 139 GCC senior executives/board directors plus 14 interviews reports 84% adoption to some extent, but only 31% scaled or fully deployed across the organization and 11% meeting its “value realizer” definition. It points to output inaccuracy, weak technology/data foundations, operating model, change management, and workflow redesign. Deloitte’s Middle East press release reports 84% have not redesigned roles/workflows and only 21% report mature autonomous-AI governance. Both are consulting claims and require cross-reference ([McKinsey evidence](research/sources/evidence/ai-production-results-04-mckinsey-gcc.md), [Deloitte evidence](research/sources/evidence/ai-production-results-05-deloitte-me.md)).

## Notable Claims Requiring Cross-Reference

- **“Only 5% of AI pilots generated millions in value.”** This appears in TechTarget’s account of an MIT study, but the direct MIT URL returned an unrelated NANDA Media Lab page when fetched. Keep this attributed to TechTarget only until the primary MIT report is verified; do not use it as an independently confirmed prevalence rate.
- **IBM’s “at least 50% abandoned after proof of concept” statistic** is attributed by IBM to Gartner. Gartner was not independently fetched here; do not present IBM’s citation as direct Gartner evidence.
- **Survey percentages are not deployment audits.** Cisco, Wharton, McKinsey, and Deloitte rely on self-reported respondents; consultancy/vendor sponsorship and differing definitions of “adoption,” “production,” “scale,” and “value” prevent direct comparison.
- **McKinsey’s 11% GCC “value realizers”** is based on a small subgroup (approximately 13 respondents), so it is directional, not a regional prevalence estimate.
- **RAND’s failure findings are qualitative and older than 18 months.** They are valuable for mechanisms and buyer questions, but the interviews occurred in 2023 and the study excludes prompt-only pretrained-LLM projects.
- **Named disappointment cases need primary postmortems.** TechTarget is secondary reporting; the McDonald’s, Amazon, and Zillow cases are older than 18 months. Treat them as documented examples of decision triggers, not evidence that a particular failure mode dominates.
- **IBM’s global-bank vignette is unnamed.** It illustrates the integration pattern but is not a verifiable organizational account.

## Source Quality Assessment (Tier1government/academic,Tier2credibleindustrysurvey/news,Tier3promotional)

- **Tier 1 — academic/research:** RAND provides the strongest mechanism-level evidence through 65 semi-structured practitioner interviews, but it is exploratory, qualitative, and older than 18 months. Wharton provides a large repeated enterprise survey with transparent sampling and dates, but it is self-reported and co-produced with GBK Collective.
- **Tier 2 — credible industry survey/news:** Cisco has unusually large, cross-market survey coverage including Saudi Arabia and the UAE, but it is vendor-sponsored and uses Cisco-defined readiness measures. TechTarget offers concrete deployment/disappointment accounts and dates, but is secondary reporting and links to an unverified MIT statistic.
- **Tier 3 — promotional/consulting:** McKinsey is the most directly GCC-relevant source and includes interviews, but is a consulting survey with self-reported data and a small value-realizer subgroup. Deloitte’s Middle East press release gives useful regional signals but omits detailed methodology. IBM’s production analysis is operationally useful, yet vendor marketing content with no independent sample and an unnamed illustrative bank.

## Gaps & Unanswered Questions

- No independently verified, current buyer interviews or procurement records were used, per instruction; the buyer situations above are evidence-led starting scenarios, not claims about the project’s existing customers.
- The research does not reveal how often a paid strategy/roadmap is completed without an implementation handoff, what roadmap deliverables buyers actually received, or which budget/approval gate blocks the next purchase.
- There is little primary evidence connecting a specific failed pilot to maintenance ownership, model monitoring costs, data refresh obligations, or a quantified repair-versus-replace decision.
- Sources differ on whether “production” means a controlled launch, one business function, or enterprise-wide deployment. A buyer-research instrument should ask for deployment scope, live users, system-of-record writes, human review, uptime/quality thresholds, and budget owner rather than accept the label.
- Direct validation of the MIT NANDA/GenAI Divide report remains outstanding; the fetched search URL resolved to a different MIT NANDA project.
- The Arabic/GCC search results surfaced regional reporting and consulting sources, but no directly fetched Arabic-language primary study. Country-level UAE, Saudi, Qatar, Bahrain, Kuwait, and Oman differences remain unanswered.
